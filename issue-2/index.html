Summer Pilot Results

Before the fall BoodleBox pilot got underway, we closed out the summer pilot with a full survey of participants — 34 faculty and staff respondents, split nearly evenly between the two groups. The short version: it went well, and the results are shaping how we're running things this fall.

Beyond satisfaction with the platform itself, the pilot also seemed to shift the conversation around AI: 75% said the experience changed how they think or talk about AI, and 88% said they became more open to discussing AI use.

The most-used feature by far was simply chatting with AI models (94% of respondents), followed by image generation and document analysis. Respondents pointed to real time savings on syllabi, correspondence, and course prep, and several highlighted the ability to switch between different AI models as a standout feature.

Equity was the standout theme. 88% of respondents agreed that BoodleBox promotes more equal AI access regardless of financial means. As one participant put it: “If we do not make it equal access, our students, already divided by economic class, will not be using the same tools.”

Not everything was smooth: performance and speed were the most common technical complaint, and 38% of respondents identified lack of time as a barrier to deeper use. Both are informing our training plans this fall, including requests for more hands-on workshop time, applied examples, and clearer beginner and advanced options.

Around HWS

There is a lot happening around AI at HWS this fall. Here are a few ways to learn more, get involved, or find support.

Fall Pilot DEC: Understanding AI & Data

BoodleBox Pilot

The BoodleBox pilot is continuing this fall, with just over 400 students across 15 courses invited to participate. The pilot gives participating students and faculty access to multiple generative AI models in one place.

Students will complete a brief orientation before their accounts are activated, including the HWS AI Literacy Modules.

Student Support

AI Drop-In Hours

AI Drop-In Hours are available for students on Tuesdays from 11:30 a.m. to 12:45 p.m. as an informal space to ask questions, troubleshoot, explore ideas, or get support with generative AI tools.

These hours are designed primarily for students, but faculty are also welcome to stop in if they would find the time useful.

Digital Learning Center

Student Workshop Series

The Digital Learning Center is offering a Student Workshop Series throughout the fall semester on Thursdays from 4:30 to 5:30 p.m. in Learning Studio 2 (LS2).

Workshops cover practical digital skills and AI topics, including AI literacy, ethical questions around AI use, hallucinations and bad prompts, Excel, video and audio editing, Photoshop, and more.

Canvas Commons DEC: Ethical & Responsible Use

HWS AI Literacy Modules

The HWS AI Literacy Modules are available in Canvas Commons for faculty to import into their courses. The four-module sequence gives students a shared foundation in understanding AI, responsible AI use, citing AI, and applying AI literacy concepts to academic situations.

Before assigning the modules, faculty are encouraged to review them in full and consider how the language, examples, and guidance align with their own course expectations and approach to AI.

Differences can be part of the learning.
You may find places where the modules differ from your own policies, disciplinary norms, or beliefs about appropriate AI use. Those differences can be a useful opportunity for conversation with students about why expectations for AI may vary across courses, assignments, disciplines, and professional contexts.

From the HWS Community

Community Perspective

The Prompt is intended to include a range of perspectives on generative AI. In this issue, Walter Bowyer shares how his concerns about AI shape the way he approaches it with students.

Walter Bowyer
DEC: Critical Thinking DEC: Human-Centricity & EQ

I believe AI is a terrible development. It will benefit a few, but will be bad for most, bad for society, and bad for the world. That said: 1. it’s here, 2. students are using it, and 3. prohibitions are ineffective (Lund et al 2025, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10805-025-09613-3). Thus, I feel compelled to help students learn to integrate AI with their own intelligences. Although I have been using it for several years, I certainly am not an expert. However, Sarah Gobe invited me to contribute a couple of my strategies.

  1. I give short in-class writing assignments to work on specific skills (e.g. concision, vividness, audience), they then critique it, then ask AI to critique (not rewrite) it. Finally, I ask them to critique AI’s critique. I find that they become very engaged when AI suggests changes to their own work.
  2. We work in class on prompt engineering. They use my suggested prompt to get AI to critique their work, then they revise the prompt and compare results. As a group we discuss the value of the AI responses resulting from various prompting strategies.
  3. Most important and most challenging is to get them to think about the ethics of AI use, especially in the context of academic honesty. While reluctant to discuss their own use, they become very engaged when asked their perceptions of how their peers use AI. After a class discussion, small groups are each assigned a scenario (from an ever-evolving list), and they are asked what they would do or how they would feel, including why, pro/con, right/wrong, etc. (Many thanks to Karen Frost-Arnold for very helpful conversations on how to stimulate thoughtful responses.)

The final reason I learn and teach AI: as Sun Tzu, the ancient Chinese military strategist famously said, “If you know the enemy …, you need not fear the battle.” (Source: Gemini.)

Try This

DEC: Critical Thinking

Ask AI to Show Its Assumptions

When we ask AI a question, it often fills in missing context on its own. Sometimes those assumptions are harmless. Sometimes they shape the answer in ways we did not intend.

Before answering my question, identify three assumptions you might be making about my request. Then ask me what additional context would make your response more useful.

Then continue with your actual question or task and see whether the additional context changes the response.

What to notice: Did the assumptions surprise you? Did adding more context change the quality of the response?

This is a simple way to slow down the interaction and make the conversation with AI a little more intentional.
