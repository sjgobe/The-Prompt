Where we are with AI this fall

Welcome Back to The Prompt

Starting with this issue, The Prompt is going out to faculty and staff across HWS.

AI keeps showing up in more parts of our work, and not everyone feels the same way about it. Some people are using it every day. Some are curious but cautious. Others have real concerns about where all of this is headed. The Prompt is meant to have room for all of those perspectives.

The Prompt should also be something we build together. If you have tried something that worked, found a resource worth sharing, have a question you keep coming back to, or want to write something yourself, please send it our way.

More than anything, this should be a place to compare notes, ask better questions, and figure some of this out together.

Summer Pilot Results

BoodleBox is an AI platform that gives users access to several leading AI models in one place, including models from ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. We chose BoodleBox because it makes it easier to explore and compare those tools within a shared, institutionally supported environment.

Before moving into the fall BoodleBox pilot, the 34 faculty and staff who participated over the summer were asked what was working, what was not, and what they wanted more of. Overall, the response was positive, but some of the most useful feedback came from hearing where people still felt stuck.

One result that stood out: 75% said the summer changed how they think or talk about AI, and 88% said they are now more willing to talk about using it. That matters. The goal was never just to give people another tool. It was also to create some space to experiment, ask questions, and talk honestly about what AI might mean for our work.

The most common use was simply talking with the different models, followed by image generation and document analysis. People also pointed to practical time savings with things like syllabi, emails, and course preparation. Several appreciated being able to compare models without jumping between separate platforms.

Equity came up again and again. 88% agreed that BoodleBox makes access to strong AI tools more equal, regardless of what someone can afford to pay. One participant put it plainly: “If we do not make it equal access, our students, already divided by economic class, will not be using the same tools.”

The feedback was not all positive, and that is important to include. Speed was the most common complaint, with users noting that the tool could be slow, get "stuck," and require a refresh. Additionally, 38% said they simply did not have enough time to go deeper. People also asked for more hands-on practice, examples they could use right away, and clearer options for beginners and more experienced users. Those requests are helping shape what we offer this fall.

There is a lot happening around AI at HWS this fall. Here are a few of the things you can use, join, or share with students.

Fall Pilot

BoodleBox Pilot

Just over 400 students across 15 courses are being invited into BoodleBox this fall. Students and faculty can work with several generative AI models in one place instead of relying on whichever individual tools they happen to have access to.

Before student accounts are activated, students complete a short BoodleBox orientation and the HWS AI Literacy Modules.

Student Support

AI Drop-In Hours

AI Drop-In Hours are held Tuesdays from 11:30 a.m. to 12:45 p.m. Students can bring a question, get help with something they are trying, or simply come in and explore. Faculty are welcome too.

Digital Learning Center

Student Workshop Series

The Digital Learning Center is offering student workshops on Thursdays from 4:30 to 5:30 p.m. in Learning Studio 2 (LS2). Some are AI-focused, including AI literacy, ethics, hallucinations, and prompting, while others cover tools like Excel, Photoshop, and video and audio editing.

Canvas Commons

HWS AI Literacy Modules

The HWS AI Literacy Modules are available in Canvas Commons and can be imported into your courses. The four modules are designed to give students a shared starting point for understanding how AI works, where it can go wrong, and how to think about responsible use.

Before assigning the modules, take a few minutes to read through them. If something does not match your own course expectations, that is worth talking about with students. Those differences can lead to useful conversations about why appropriate AI use changes across courses, disciplines, and workplaces.

From the HWS Community

Community Perspectives

This section makes room for the different ways people at HWS are thinking about AI. In this issue, Walter Bowyer writes about why he is teaching with AI even though he has serious concerns about it, and Melissa Hosek reflects on what happened when AI made it surprisingly easy to build an educational game.

Walter Bowyer

I believe AI is a terrible development. It will benefit a few, but will be bad for most, bad for society, and bad for the world. That said: 1. it’s here, 2. students are using it, and 3. prohibitions are ineffective (Lund et al 2025, doi.org/10.1007/s10805-025-09613-3). Thus, I feel compelled to help students learn to integrate AI with their own intelligences. Although I have been using it for several years, I certainly am not an expert. However, Sarah Gobe invited me to contribute a couple of my strategies.

  1. I give short in-class writing assignments to work on specific skills (e.g. concision, vividness, audience), they then critique it, then ask AI to critique (not rewrite) it. Finally, I ask them to critique AI’s critique. I find that they become very engaged when AI suggests changes to their own work.
  2. We work in class on prompt engineering. They use my suggested prompt to get AI to critique their work, then they revise the prompt and compare results. As a group we discuss the value of the AI responses resulting from various prompting strategies.
  3. Most important and most challenging is to get them to think about the ethics of AI use, especially in the context of academic honesty. While reluctant to discuss their own use, they become very engaged when asked their perceptions of how their peers use AI. After a class discussion, small groups are each assigned a scenario (from an ever-evolving list), and they are asked what they would do or how they would feel, including why, pro/con, right/wrong, etc. (Many thanks to Karen Frost-Arnold for very helpful conversations on how to stimulate thoughtful responses.)

The final reason I learn and teach AI: as Sun Tzu, the ancient Chinese military strategist famously said, “If you know the enemy …, you need not fear the battle.” (Source: Gemini.)

Reflections on AI Integration through Simple Game Development

Melissa A. Hosek • September 2026

With AI-assisted coding (sometimes known as “vibe coding”), it is easier than ever to create a digital game for educational purposes. Never was this more true than when Anthropic’s Fable 5 LLM was freely available to users. Using ClaudeCode, you could type in a simple prompt (as I did) like “Build a web-browser game in the style of Sherlock Holmes to teach students about file management practices and concepts.” Then, the powerful, frontier model would create a development plan for my approval and, with the click of a button, charge multiple agents with progressing through the coding workflow.

By default, Claude pauses before and after tasks for manual human approval before moving on to the next task, but you could easily click the “auto” button to automatically approve any decision made by the agent. My instructor at the Digital Humanities Summer Institute called this “YOLO” mode: you gamble that the agents will make good decisions, and in exchange, you don’t have to bother with monitoring the code-building process. In a matter of 30 minutes, the first draft of the educational game that “I” built was complete and live without me typing out any code whatsoever.

Fable 5 is no longer free; you have to pay for the number of tokens you use. Since I refuse to pay more than my current subscription, that means it is off limits to me. But even on a “smaller” (still very big!) model like Opus4.6 or Opus5, the workflow for AI-assisted coding is essentially the same: create the prompt, verify a plan, approve agentic decisions. You may be prompted to answer more questions and clarify your vision than you would have with Fable, but for a simple product like a web browser game, you will likely end up with an outcome of comparable quality. Frontier AI tools are pretty good at being mediocre coders.

You might think that now is your chance to build games that will inspire your students. The tools are at your fingertips! But I would caution that just because educational content can be turned into a game does not mean that it facilitates good learning. We can look to past and ongoing practices to consider what to integrate into our teaching.

Generally speaking, I have mixed feelings about gamification as a pedagogical approach, especially where ed-tech is concerned. Some of the most inane and gimmicky educational products are, in reality, little more than entertainment. Take, for example, Duolingo (which now has an integration in LinkedIn to show off your current streak). While I support any form of entertainment that piques interest in language and culture, I would not endorse Duolingo for meaningful language learning. It excels at introducing new vocabularies and sentence structures but fails to encourage the development of language skills beyond the intermediate level (such as explaining a problem, narrating in various tenses, and explaining a situation using extended discourse). Duolingo, like other ed-tech products, is designed to attract and maintain engagement, but not necessarily for guiding students through the various stages of the learning process. As one report from the American Psychological Association cautions, we must not confuse engagement with learning.

Fortunately, there are examples that demonstrate the strength of using games in teaching. For example, Brad Cosentino’s SquirrelMapper project features several engaging games that introduce players to his research subject and the practices of citizen science. Quinn Daedal, a digital humanities specialist at Stanford University, used a semester-long Role-Playing-Game to walk students through the process of applying for, developing, and collaborating on major grant projects. Effective gamification is, thus, developed by effective teachers with a clear vision of learning goals.

So, here’s what I can tell you about my experience building an educational game:

I made a few simple revisions but largely left the design and contents up to Fable 5. If you get the impression that some of its elements reek of AI, you’re not wrong. I wanted to establish a baseline understanding of what could be made with minimal human-in-the-loop. The intended learning goal is very simple: introduce good file management practices in a way that is accessible and mildly entertaining. It’s neither profound nor critical, but it should be pleasant at the very least. I encourage you to try it and develop your own opinion about whether something like this could bring value to your teaching.

Try This

Ask AI to Show Its Assumptions

AI fills in a lot of blanks when we do not give it enough context. Sometimes that is harmless. Sometimes it changes the answer in ways we did not intend. This simple prompt can help make some of those hidden assumptions a little more visible.

Before answering my question, identify three assumptions you might be making about my request. Then ask me what additional context would make your response more useful.

Then ask your actual question and see what changes once you answer the follow-up.

Pay attention to: What did the AI assume about you, your goal, or your context? Did correcting any of those assumptions improve the response?

It only adds a minute or two, but it can make the conversation much more useful.

Worth a Look

AI Engagement Guide

If you are still working through how to describe acceptable AI use on an assignment, this tool from Finger Lakes Community College is worth a look. It walks you through different levels of AI use, from no AI to much more open use, and helps you think through what students should disclose.

Once you make your selections, it generates plain-language wording that you can adapt for a syllabus or assignment. It is especially useful because it gives us something more specific than simply saying AI is either “allowed” or “not allowed.”
